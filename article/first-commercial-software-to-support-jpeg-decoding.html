First Commercial Software to Support JPEG Decoding

This article explores the origins of digital image compression software, specifically identifying the earliest commercial software program capable of decoding JPEG files. While the JPEG standard was formally finalized in 1992, software developers were actively implementing draft specifications years prior. Storm Technology’s PicturePress, released in late 1990 for the Apple Macintosh, is widely recognized as the very first commercial software application to decode and encode JPEG images.

The Dawn of PicturePress

In October 1990, a Silicon Valley startup named Storm Technology released PicturePress. Developed specifically for Apple Macintosh computers, PicturePress was designed to compress and decompress 24-bit color photographic images. At the time, uncompressed high-resolution color images consumed vast amounts of memory and storage, often overwhelming standard hard drives of the era.

PicturePress implemented the emerging baseline standard developed by the Joint Photographic Experts Group (JPEG), a joint committee formed by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) and the International Telegraph and Telephone Consultative Committee (CCITT). Although the formal international standard (ISO/IEC 10918-1 / ITU-T T.81) was not fully ratified until 1992, PicturePress utilized the group’s draft Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) lossy compression algorithms, making it the pioneer in commercial JPEG implementation.

Features and Integration

PicturePress was distributed as both a standalone software application and an external plug-in for Adobe Photoshop. Because 1990-era microprocessors struggled with the mathematically intensive calculations required by the JPEG Discrete Cosine Transform, PicturePress featured two modes of operation:

  • Pure Software Decoding: Users could decode and view JPEG files directly on their standard Macintosh systems, though processing larger images could take several seconds to minutes depending on the CPU.
  • Hardware-Accelerated Decoding: Storm Technology simultaneously sold the "PicturePress Accelerator," a NuBus expansion board featuring a dedicated AT&T WE DSP32C digital signal processor to offload decoding and encoding calculations from the host processor.

Historical Context and Successors

Prior to PicturePress, digital image decoding was restricted to proprietary, non-standard compression formats or academic and laboratory tools. Storm Technology demonstrated that standardized, transform-based compression could be sold as a commercially viable consumer and desktop publishing tool.

Following PicturePress's introduction, mainstream adoption accelerated. In December 1991, Apple released QuickTime 1.0, which integrated native JPEG decompression into the operating system architecture. By 1993, mainstream graphics software—such as Adobe Photoshop 2.5—and early web browsers began incorporating native JPEG software decoders directly into their core codebases, establishing JPEG as the dominant photographic format of the digital era.
