Why ITU-T and ISO Collaborated to Create JPEG

The collaboration between the ITU-T and ISO to establish the Joint Photographic Experts Group (JPEG) was driven by the urgent need for a unified digital image compression standard in the late 1980s. Facing the emergence of conflicting standards between telecommunications networks and computer systems, both organizations joined forces to avoid market fragmentation and engineer a single, globally interoperable compression method. This article examines the technological demands that brought both bodies together, the strategic necessity of their partnership, and the lasting impact of their joint technical standard.

Divergent Industry Needs

During the mid-1980s, the computing and telecommunications sectors were rapidly modernizing, yet they operated in distinct technical silos:

  • Telecommunications (ITU-T / CCITT): The International Telegraph and Telephone Consultative Committee (CCITT, now ITU-T) focused on transmitting data across bandwidth-constrained networks, such as Integrated Services Digital Networks (ISDN), facsimile, and videotex systems. They required a method to transmit still, full-color photographs quickly over telephone lines.
  • Information Technology (ISO/IEC): The International Organization for Standardization (ISO), working alongside the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC), was addressing computer-based storage, graphical displays, and digital media (such as CD-ROMs). Their priority was efficient file storage and high-fidelity rendering on personal computers and workstations.

Both sectors realized that uncompressed continuous-tone color images required massive amounts of memory and bandwidth that existing infrastructure could not support.

The Threat of Market Fragmentation

Because both bodies were pursuing compression algorithms simultaneously, the technology industry faced the risk of duplicate, incompatible standards. A format optimized purely for telecommunications transmission would likely fail to serve the file-structure requirements of computer operating systems, while a computing standard might be too computationally demanding or unsuitable for streaming over telephone networks.

Had ISO and CCITT developed competing formats, hardware manufacturers, software developers, and telecommunications operators would have been forced to build costly converters or choose between divided ecosystems. Recognizing this mutual challenge, leaders from both organizations decided that a joint international effort was preferable to a standards war.

The Formation of the JPEG Committee

In 1986, the two organizations formally established the Joint Photographic Experts Group. Organizationally, this brought together CCITT Study Group VIII and ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 2/WG 8 (later under SC 29).

The joint committee pooled engineering expertise from telecommunications labs, academic institutions, and computer manufacturers worldwide. Together, they evaluated several competing compression techniques before selecting an algorithm based on the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT). By pooling technical and testing resources, the group ensured the chosen algorithm balanced processing speed, compression ratios, and visual quality across both transmission and storage environments.

The Lasting Impact

The collaboration concluded with the formal release of the standard in 1992, published identically as ITU-T Recommendation T.81 and ISO/IEC 10918-1.

By working together instead of competing, the ITU-T and ISO ensured that the JPEG standard became universal. The format allowed digital cameras, web browsers, personal computers, and telecommunications equipment to exchange photographic images seamlessly, providing the foundational technology that enabled the visual expansion of the early World Wide Web and modern digital photography.
