How JPEG Patent Threats Accelerated PNG Adoption

In the late 1990s, emerging legal disputes and patent uncertainties surrounding web graphics created an urgent demand for a completely unencumbered image format. While the Portable Network Graphics (PNG) format was initially created to replace patent-burdened GIFs, rising intellectual property claims and licensing anxieties around JPEG compression technologies further motivated browser vendors, standards bodies, and developers to embrace and accelerate the adoption of PNG as a legally safe alternative.

The Fragile State of Early Web Imagery

During the mid-1990s, the World Wide Web relied almost exclusively on two image formats: GIF for graphics with few colors and JPEG for photographs. The fragility of this ecosystem became apparent when Unisys began aggressively enforcing licensing fees for the LZW compression algorithm used in GIFs.

This controversy made the tech industry hyper-aware of intellectual property risks in core web standards. Consequently, software developers and standards organizations turned their scrutiny toward JPEG, seeking to determine whether it, too, carried hidden legal liabilities.

Patent Ambiguities Surrounding JPEG

Although the JPEG format was designed by the Joint Photographic Experts Group to be an open standard, its legal status was far from clean. Several factors introduced serious uncertainty:

  • Arithmetic Coding Restrictions: The full JPEG specification included arithmetic coding as an option for superior compression. However, this feature was heavily covered by patents owned by companies like IBM, AT&T, and Mitsubishi, forcing the web to rely strictly on the less efficient Huffman coding.
  • Submarine Patent Threats: In the late 1990s, rumors and early claims emerged regarding third-party patents that allegedly covered core aspects of the baseline Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) algorithms used in JPEG. These fears materialized more explicitly when companies like Forgent Networks began asserting patent rights over standard JPEG decoders.
  • Licensing Liabilities for Software Developers: Operating system vendors and browser developers faced the prospect of retroactive royalties or expensive litigation if JPEG was found to infringe on proprietary compression patents.

PNG as the Patent-Proof Haven

Developed by the PNG Development Group and endorsed by the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C), PNG was deliberately architected to be completely immune to intellectual property claims.

The format avoided proprietary compression entirely by using the Deflate algorithm, an open, patent-unencumbered implementation found in the zlib library. Because PNG's creators documented their patent clearance and explicitly chose royalty-free mathematical techniques, the format offered legal certainty that neither GIF nor JPEG could guarantee at the time.

Acceleration by Standards Bodies and Browsers

Faced with unpredictable licensing exposure across existing web media, key industry players moved quickly to support PNG:

  1. W3C Recommendation: The W3C finalized PNG as an official recommendation in October 1996, actively urging developers to migrate away from formats fraught with patent risks.
  2. Browser Integration: Major web browsers, including Netscape Navigator and Microsoft Internet Explorer, accelerated native integration of PNG decoders to ensure their platforms could display images without exposure to compression-related lawsuits.
  3. Open-Source Adoption: The open-source community rapidly integrated libpng into Linux distributions, web servers, and graphics suites, ensuring that infrastructure software remained free from proprietary encumbrances.

The legal vulnerabilities that rattled the digital imaging landscape in the late 1990s proved that technical superiority meant little without legal safety. By offering guaranteed royalty-free status alongside modern features like alpha transparency and lossless compression, PNG became a cornerstone of web architecture.
