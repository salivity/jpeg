JPEG XL vs HEIC: The Role of Royalty-Free Licensing

The battle between JPEG XL and HEIC for the future of digital imaging is defined as much by intellectual property law as it is by compression efficiency. While both modern image formats offer high dynamic range (HDR), superior compression ratios, and wide color gamuts compared to legacy JPEG, their licensing structures create fundamentally divergent paths to market adoption. HEIC is encumbered by complex, costly patent pools, whereas JPEG XL provides a fully royalty-free, open-source alternative designed specifically to eliminate legal risks for developers, hardware vendors, and the open web.

The Licensing Divide: Open Standards vs. Patent Pools

JPEG XL was engineered by the Joint Photographic Experts Group as an open, royalty-free standard with a reference implementation published under the Apache 2.0 license. Anyone can build, distribute, and monetize software or hardware utilizing JPEG XL without paying licensing fees or entering into bilateral intellectual property agreements.

HEIC, which packages images encoded via the High Efficiency Video Coding (HEVC/H.265) standard inside the High Efficiency Image File Format (HEIF), suffers from a notoriously fragmented patent landscape. Multiple competing patent pools—including Access Advance, MPEG LA (now Via Licensing Alliance), and Velos Media—claim rights to HEVC technology. This creates an ongoing risk of unpredictable royalty demands, ambiguous terms, and separate per-unit fees for software distribution.

The Impact on Web Browsers and Open-Source Software

Royalty-free licensing is essential for the open web. Major open-source web browsers and operating systems (such as Chromium, Mozilla Firefox, and Linux distributions) have historically resisted native HEIC support. Distributing proprietary codecs that require per-installation royalties or indemnity risks conflicts directly with the open-source ethos and business models of these platforms.

Because JPEG XL is royalty-free, software vendors can incorporate native decoders and encoders directly into their products without legal friction. While browser adoption for JPEG XL has faced strategic hurdles, its legal structure allows any platform to deploy it universally without paying a toll to patent holders. In contrast, HEIC’s licensing model actively prevents it from ever becoming an unrestricted, ubiquitous web format.

Corporate Ecosystems and Hardware Acceleration

HEIC found early success primarily because Apple possessed existing HEVC patent rights and hardware-accelerated HEVC decoders built into its silicon. For large tech conglomerates already paying HEVC royalties for video streaming and camera capture, deploying HEIC across their hardware ecosystem required minimal incremental legal overhead.

However, for third-party developers, web services, and cloud storage providers, processing HEIC files requires specialized server-side transcoders and legal compliance reviews. This has fragmented the user experience, forcing web platforms to convert HEIC images to standard JPEG before displaying them to users on non-Apple devices. JPEG XL bypasses this friction entirely, enabling end-to-end processing across heterogeneous environments at zero licensing cost.

The Deciding Factor in the Format Wars

Technical benchmarks between JPEG XL and HEIC demonstrate comparable compression efficiency and feature parity. As a result, the competitive landscape hinges entirely on accessibility. HEIC’s proprietary encumbrances restrict it to a siloed format optimized for consumer capture devices within closed ecosystems. Royalty-free licensing positions JPEG XL as the viable universal standard for the open web, archival workflows, and cross-platform media delivery.
