When Was the First JPEG Standard Published?

The JPEG format revolutionized digital photography and web graphics by providing an efficient, standardized method for compressing continuous-tone still images. This article explores the release timeline of the first official JPEG standard, designated as ISO/IEC 10918-1, detailing when it was officially adopted, the organizations behind its creation, and why it became the foundation of modern digital imaging.

The first official JPEG standard, titled ISO/IEC 10918-1 (Information technology — Digital compression and coding of continuous-tone still images: Requirements and guidelines), was officially published by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) in 1994. Prior to this, the core specification was formally approved and published as ITU-T Recommendation T.81 by the International Telecommunication Union (formerly CCITT) in September 1992.

The standard was developed by the Joint Photographic Experts Group, a collaborative working group formed in 1986 that brought together experts from the ISO, IEC, and CCITT. Their objective was to solve a critical limitation of early computing: uncompressed digital image files were too massive to store easily or transmit across the slow networks of the era.

At the heart of the standard was the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) compression algorithm. This lossy compression technique selectively discarded visual data that the human eye cannot easily perceive, dramatically shrinking file sizes without causing noticeable drops in perceived quality. The specification also detailed lossless encoding modes, baseline sequential operations, and progressive display capabilities.

Following its adoption between 1992 and 1994, the JPEG standard quickly became the global benchmark for digital photography and the burgeoning World Wide Web. By making high-resolution color images practical to download and display, ISO/IEC 10918-1 transformed internet media and remains one of the most widely used digital standards in the world today.
